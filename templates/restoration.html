Restore Before Replace

A roof that has moved beyond routine repair may still have useful life remaining.

Roof restoration is often the next step when repairs alone are no longer the best long-term answer, but the existing roof has not deteriorated enough to require replacement.

The process combines necessary repairs with a protective restoration system designed to extend the roof's service life, improve weather resistance, and protect the existing membrane from continued ultraviolet exposure.

For the right roof, restoration can avoid the cost, disruption, labor, equipment, and waste associated with tearing off and replacing the entire roofing system.

Protection From UV Exposure

Sunlight is one of the most persistent deteriorating forces acting on a roof.

Roofing materials are exposed to ultraviolet radiation every day. Over time, that exposure contributes to drying, surface erosion, loss of flexibility, cracking, and deterioration of the original roof membrane.

A restoration system creates a new protective surface over the existing roof. That surface reflects sunlight and helps reduce the continued UV exposure reaching the original membrane.

The condition of the roof still matters. If deterioration has progressed too far, coating the surface will not correct the underlying failure. That is why restoration must begin with a thorough evaluation rather than simply applying material over an aging roof.

01

Restoration protects the roof that is already there.

The goal is not to hide deterioration. It is to repair serviceable deficiencies and add a protective system before the existing roof moves beyond the restoration window.

Is the Roof a Candidate?

Restoration only works when the existing roof is still capable of supporting it.

The roof must be evaluated for membrane condition, moisture, adhesion, drainage, flashing performance, surface contamination, and the extent of existing deterioration.

Excessive wet insulation can prevent restoration from being a practical option. Trapped moisture must be identified, and isolated wet areas may need to be removed and replaced before restoration work can begin.

Severe membrane deterioration, widespread delamination, structural concerns, chronic drainage problems, or repeated failures throughout the roof may also indicate that replacement would provide better long-term value.

A proper evaluation determines whether the roof falls within the restoration window and what repairs or preparation would be required before the protective system is installed.

Why Restore a Roof?

A practical alternative when the roof is too far along for simple repair but not ready for replacement

The value of restoration comes from extending service life while preserving as much of the existing roofing investment as possible.

01

Extend Roof Life

A properly designed restoration system can provide additional service life to a roof that remains structurally and functionally serviceable.

02

Reduce Replacement Cost

Restoration can often save approximately 45% to 50% compared with full roof replacement by avoiding tear-off and much of the labor and equipment associated with replacement.

03

Improve UV Protection

The restored surface helps protect the original roof membrane from continued ultraviolet exposure and related surface deterioration.

04

Avoid Tear-Off

Keeping the existing roof in place can reduce demolition, disposal, rooftop disruption, equipment needs, and the amount of material sent to a landfill.

05

Preserve Future Options

Because restoration is not generally treated as adding a new roof layer, it may preserve future reroofing options under applicable building-code limitations.

06

Warranty Options

Depending on the roof system, preparation, product, reinforcement, and number of coats, restoration warranties may range from five to fifteen years.

Roof Systems That May Be Restored

Restoration is primarily an option for commercial low-slope and metal roofing systems.

Many single-ply membranes, modified bitumen roofs, built-up roofs, spray polyurethane foam systems, and metal roofs may be candidates for restoration when their condition and construction allow it.

Shingle, tile, and slate roofing systems do not typically fall within this type of fluid-applied commercial roof restoration. Those systems generally require repair or replacement using materials and methods specific to the original roof.

Compatibility between the existing surface and the proposed restoration material must also be confirmed. Adhesion testing, cleaning requirements, primers, and manufacturer specifications all influence the final restoration approach.

Different Roofs Require Different Systems

The restoration assembly must match the roof, its condition, and how water moves across it.

Metal roofs may often be restored by repairing fasteners, seams, penetrations, and flashing details before applying the specified coating system.

Low-slope membrane roofs may require more extensive preparation, localized membrane replacement, reinforced seams and flashings, or a fully reinforced restoration system.

Roofs that experience areas of standing water may require a reinforced application and greater finished coating thickness. The system must be selected and installed with those conditions in mind so the restored surface can resist premature deterioration.

Restoration work may include:

  • Roof cleaning and surface preparation
  • Adhesion testing and manufacturer review
  • Removal and replacement of isolated wet areas
  • Membrane, seam, and flashing repairs
  • Fastener and metal-panel detail repairs
  • Reinforcement at seams and transitions
  • Base and finish coating applications
  • Final inspection and warranty documentation
